# Weight loss at about 1600 kcal/day for a 50-year-old man: evidence brief

Compiled 2026-11-07 by a research agent. nice.org.uk and nhs.uk could not be fetched directly, so NICE and NHS statements rest on secondary mirrors and should be checked against the primary pages before quoting.

## 1. Energy needs and whether 1600 kcal is sensible

Mifflin-St Jeor (male): 10 x 96 + 6.25 x 177.8 - 5 x 50 + 5 = **1826 kcal/day BMR**. Frankenfield 2005 found this the most reliable common equation, within 10% of measured RMR most often, but individual error can exceed 10%.

- TDEE sedentary (x1.2): about 2190 kcal
- TDEE lightly active (x1.375): about 2510 kcal

1600 kcal is a deficit of about 590 (sedentary) to 910 (lightly active) kcal, giving 0.5 to 0.85 kg/week on the 7700 kcal/kg heuristic, inside the 0.5 to 1 kg/week range used by NHS Better Health. It sits at the top of the Obesity Medicine Association's low-calorie band for men (1200 to 1600) and at the bottom of the 1500 to 1800 range cited as safe for men in the 2024 Obesity Society consensus. The NHS 12-week plan defaults most men to 1900 kcal/day. NICE NG246 (Jan 2025, updated Jan 2026) recommends flexible, balanced energy restriction and confines 800 to 1200 kcal diets to supervised services.

**Verdict**: 1600 is defensible for a 96 kg sedentary man but is at the aggressive end, with little margin for under-reporting or added exercise.

**When 1600 becomes too low.** Net running cost is about 1 kcal/kg/km: about 480 kcal per 5 km at 96 kg. Three 5 km runs a week averages about 200 kcal/day. Once running is established, the deficit on 1600 approaches 1000 kcal/day and energy availability on run days falls to roughly 17 kcal/kg fat-free mass, well below the 30 kcal/kg level the 2023 IOC REDs consensus describes as substantially perturbing physiology (male thresholds remain uncertain). Practical rule: once running 3 times a week, eat about 1800 to 1900 on run days, or cap the average deficit at about 750 kcal/day. At 80 kg his BMR is about 1670 and sedentary TDEE about 2000, so 1600 becomes a mild deficit automatically.

## 2. Protein

- PROT-AGE 2013 and ESPEN 2022: at least 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg/day for older adults, 1.2 to 1.5 g/kg with active weight loss. Aimed at over-65s but routinely extrapolated to 50+.
- Leidy 2015 (AJCN): 1.2 to 1.6 g/kg/day during weight loss, 25 to 30 g or more per meal.
- ISSN 2017: 1.4 to 2.0 g/kg/day for active people. Higher figures (2.3 to 3.1 g/kg fat-free mass) derive from lean trained populations and are not needed here.
- Hudson 2022 (pooled, n=207): same fat loss on higher vs lower protein, less lean-mass loss on higher protein, and exercise is required for the benefit. Sardeli 2018: resistance training prevented about 94% of calorie-restriction lean-mass loss in obese older adults. Resistance training is the stronger lever; protein is second.

**Numbers for him.** 1.2 to 1.6 g/kg current weight: 115 to 155 g/day. A practical anchor is 1.6 g/kg of target weight (80 kg): about 130 g/day now, scaling to 95 to 130 g at 80 kg. 130 g is 520 kcal, about a third of a 1600 kcal budget. Distribution: older muscle needs about 0.4 g/kg per meal (Moore 2015), roughly 30 to 40 g protein per meal across 3 to 4 meals. Evidence that even distribution beats skewed is mixed; treat it as a sensible default. Caveat: eGFR under 30 should not raise protein without medical advice.

## 3. Fibre

SACN 2015 recommends 30 g/day. NDNS 2019 to 2023 (published June 2025): only 4% of adults meet it; men average about 20 to 21 g/day. Reynolds 2019 (Lancet; 58 trials, 185 cohorts): higher fibre gave lower body weight, systolic BP and total cholesterol in trials, with greatest risk reduction at 25 to 29 g/day. For LDL, viscous fibre matters most (psyllium about 10 g/day gives -0.33 mmol/L). Weight effects of fibre alone are small; fibre matters more for satiety, bowel and lipids.

## 4. Diet pattern evidence

- **Low-carb vs low-fat (DIETFITS, Gardner 2018, n=609, 12 months)**: -6.0 vs -5.3 kg, difference not significant, no genotype or insulin interaction. Within-group range -30 to +10 kg. Adherence and diet quality predicted loss, not macronutrient ratio (Hauser 2024).
- **Mediterranean**: modest advantage over low-fat, similar to other diets for weight; strongest for cardiovascular outcomes. PREDIMED-Plus 2023: energy-reduced Mediterranean diet plus activity preserved about 0.6 kg more lean mass and cut visceral fat at 3 years.
- **Higher protein**: small, compliance-dependent advantage.
- **Time-restricted eating**: Liu 2022 (NEJM, 12 months) TRE plus calorie restriction no better than calorie restriction alone. Lin 2023 (Annals, 12 months): 8-hour TRE without counting matched 25% calorie restriction, with better adherence. An adherence tool, not a metabolic advantage.
- **Ultra-processed food**: Hall 2019 (inpatient crossover, n=20): +508 kcal/day and +0.9 kg in 2 weeks on UPF. Dicken 2025 (UK, n=55, 8 weeks, Eatwell-matched): -2.06% vs -1.05% weight on minimally processed vs UPF, better craving control. Small, consistent direction.

**What predicts adherence**: adherence itself predicts loss more than diet type. Adherence is higher when rules are simple, the diet matches preference, protein and fibre are high, and food is minimally processed. No macronutrient pattern is shown superior at 12 months.

## 5. Meal timing and myths

- **Breakfast**: Sievert 2019 (BMJ meta-analysis of RCTs): breakfast eaters consumed about 260 kcal/day more. No support for "breakfast boosts metabolism". Eat it if it stops you overeating later; skip it if not.
- **Late eating**: Vujovic 2022 (n=16, controlled crossover): isocaloric late eating increased hunger and reduced waketime energy expenditure. Plausible, small. "Night calories are stored as fat" is unproven at matched intake.
- **Meal frequency**: no robust effect on weight at matched calories; per-meal protein is the main reason to eat 3 to 4 times.

## 6. Hunger management

- **Protein**: acute satiety modest; long-term benefit via lean mass and adherence.
- **Fibre and volume (low energy density)**: one of the better-replicated satiety levers.
- **Water preload**: Parretti 2015 (UK primary care RCT, n=84): 500 ml before meals, -1.3 kg at 12 weeks unadjusted; weak evidence, cheap.
- **Sleep**: Zhu 2019 (41 RCTs): sleep restriction raised intake about 250 kcal/day. Tasali 2022 (JAMA Intern Med, n=80): extending sleep by about 1.2 h cut intake by 270 kcal/day in short sleepers. The strongest non-diet lever.
- **Alcohol**: 7 kcal/g, poor satiety, suppresses fat oxidation. On 1600 kcal the issue is arithmetic: two pints is about 400 kcal.

## 7. Timeline, plateaus and metabolic adaptation

Target under 79 kg (BMI under 25) is about 17 kg. At 0.5 kg/week about 34 weeks; at 0.75 kg/week about 23 weeks. Realistically 8 to 12 months because the deficit shrinks as TDEE falls, early loss includes water and glycogen, and plateaus happen. Thomas 2014 and Hall's modelling attribute the typical 6-month plateau mainly to progressive intermittent non-adherence, not metabolism. Metabolic adaptation is real but modest at moderate losses: Martins (UAB) about -92 kcal/day after 8 weeks of restriction, shrinking to about -38 kcal/day after 4 weeks of weight stability, with wide variation and no predictive value for regain. The -500 kcal/day figure (Fothergill 2016) is from Biggest Loser contestants and should not be generalised. Slower loss (up to about 0.7 to 1% body weight a week) with resistance training favours lean-mass retention.

## 8. Tracking

Self-weighing added to programmes gives about -1.7 kg extra (Madigan 2015), no significant daily vs weekly difference. Logging frequency correlates with loss in app data, confounded by motivation. UK foods: Nutracheck has a curated UK retail database; MyFitnessPal is largest but largely user-generated; Cronometer is lab-verified but weaker on UK brands. No independent UK accuracy audit exists. Hevy covers strength logging. Watch the weekly weight trend, not daily readings.
