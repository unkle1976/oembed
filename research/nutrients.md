# Nutritional repair on 1600 kcal: UK evidence brief

Compiled 2026-11-07 by a research agent. gov.uk, PMC and several journal sites were blocked from the session, so NDNS figures come from search-indexed summaries rather than primary tables. Flagged where unverified.

## 1. What UK men on a poor diet are short of (NDNS 2019 to 2023, published June 2025)

| Nutrient | NDNS finding, adults 19 to 64 | UK RNI, men 50 to 64 |
|---|---|---|
| Fibre | Mean below 30 g in every age group; only about 4% of adults meet 30 g. Men average about 20 g | 30 g/day (SACN 2015) |
| Potassium | 28% below LRNI, up from 16% in 2008 to 2010 | 3500 mg |
| Selenium | 45% below LRNI (38% in 2008 to 2010) | 75 ug |
| Iodine | Median urinary iodine 89 ug/L, below the WHO 100 ug/L threshold | 140 ug |
| Zinc | About 10% below LRNI | 9.5 mg |
| Magnesium | Earlier NDNS: about 15% of men 20 to 59 below LRNI | 300 mg |
| Folate | 10% below LRNI, mainly women | 200 ug |
| Vitamin D | 18% deficient (under 25 nmol/L), 31% in winter | 10 ug (SACN 2016) |
| Omega-3 / oily fish | Well below 1 portion a week in all adult groups | 1 portion (140 g) oily fish a week |
| Salt | 8.4 g/day adults, men 9.2 g (2018 to 2019 urinary sodium survey) | Max 6 g/day |

Caveats: NDNS energy under-reporting is about 30%, so intake shortfalls are somewhat overstated. Selenium and potassium are the two gaps that have clearly worsened. A year of takeaways and UPF puts someone at the poor end of all of these, plus very low fibre and high salt, saturated fat and free sugars.

## 2. What is hardest to hit on 1600 kcal

1600 kcal is roughly 70% of this man's maintenance, so nutrient density is not optional. Hardest to hit: fibre (30 g needs a plant staple at most meals), potassium (3500 mg needs 8 to 10 portions of vegetables, fruit, legumes or dairy daily), magnesium, selenium, iodine, omega-3 and vitamin D (effectively impossible from UK food in winter). Protein also needs active planning.

Highest nutrient-per-calorie closers:
- Fibre, potassium, magnesium, folate: lentils and chickpeas (about 8 g fibre per 100 g cooked), oats, frozen peas, spinach, broccoli, beans, wholegrain bread, berries, bananas, potatoes with skin.
- Selenium: tinned fish, eggs, wholemeal bread, two Brazil nuts (about 100 ug; do not exceed a few a day).
- Iodine: milk and yogurt (the main UK source), white fish, eggs.
- Omega-3 and vitamin D: tinned sardines, mackerel, salmon. One 120 g tin of sardines is about 1.5 to 2 g EPA+DHA and about 5 ug vitamin D.
- Protein at low calorie cost: Greek yogurt (0 to 5% fat), eggs, tinned fish, chicken breast, cottage cheese, lentils.

## 3. Vitamin D, starting in November

SACN 2016 set an RNI of 10 ug (400 IU)/day for everyone over 4. NHS advice: everyone should consider 10 ug daily from October to early March; adults should not exceed 100 ug (4000 IU)/day. The evidence base is bone and muscle; SACN judged evidence for cardiovascular and other outcomes insufficient. VITAL (2000 IU/day) and D-Health (60,000 IU/month) found no reduction in fractures, falls, cancer or mortality in mostly sufficient populations; a 2026 UK Biobank analysis (Wang et al., Clinical Nutrition) suggests benefit is plausible only in the deficient. Practical reading: 10 to 25 ug (400 to 1000 IU)/day is reasonable for him; 10 is the official floor, 25 is common and well within the upper limit. Testing is not needed to start.

## 4. Supplements: evidence vs skip

**Worth taking**
- Vitamin D 10 to 25 ug/day, autumn and winter at least.
- Creatine monohydrate 3 to 5 g/day if strength training. Meta-analyses in older adults (Chilibeck 2017, 22 RCTs; Forbes/Candow 2021) show greater lean mass and upper-body strength gains with creatine plus resistance training than training alone. Safety: Naeini 2025 (BMC Nephrology, 21 studies) found a small rise in serum creatinine (a lab artefact) and no change in GFR. No loading phase needed. Expect 1 to 2 kg water weight initially, which matters psychologically on the scales. Tell the GP before kidney blood tests.
- Psyllium husk about 10 g/day: lowers LDL by 0.33 mmol/L (Jovanovski 2018) and helps reach the fibre target. Not a substitute for food fibre.

**Conditional**
- Omega-3 EPA/DHA: eat the fish. If he will not eat oily fish 1 to 2 times a week, 1 g/day or less is a defensible fallback, but primary prevention outcome evidence is weak (VITAL and ASCEND null). AF signal: Chen 2024 (BMJ Medicine, UK Biobank, observational) found 13% higher AF in healthy regular users; a 2026 meta-analysis of 35 RCTs found AF increased only at over 1.5 g/day in high-risk people.
- Magnesium: only if intake is clearly low or BP is raised. Argeros 2025 (Hypertension, 38 RCTs): -2.8/-2.1 mmHg overall, larger in treated hypertensives, no effect in normotensives. Food is the better route; 200 to 300 mg citrate or glycinate is harmless.
- Multivitamin: weak evidence. USPSTF 2022 insufficient evidence; COSMOS 2022 null; Loftfield 2024 (390,124 adults) no mortality benefit. A cheap one-a-day at 100% RNI or less is low-risk insurance during a deficit, not where the gains are.

**Skip**
- High-dose vitamin E: SELECT 2011 found 17% higher prostate cancer in men. USPSTF recommends against.
- Beta-carotene supplements: USPSTF D recommendation (harm in smokers and ex-smokers).
- Red yeast rice: unregulated statin with variable dose; the 2024 Kobayashi contamination caused kidney injury and deaths. If LDL is high, a prescribed statin is cheaper and regulated.
- High-dose niacin: Ferrell 2024 (Nature Medicine) linked the metabolite 4PY to roughly double MACE risk; niacin trials already showed no benefit on statins.

## 5. Gut microbiome after a UPF diet: what is real

Real: diet changes shift microbiome composition within days to weeks. Wastyk 2021 (Cell, Stanford, n=36, 10 weeks): a high-fermented-food diet (about 6 servings/day) increased diversity and reduced 19 inflammatory markers; the high-fibre arm did not increase diversity but raised fibre-degrading capacity. A 2025 follow-up preprint from the same group reports the same pattern.

Hype or unproven: specific probiotic products for a healthy person; microbiome testing to personalise diet; a fixed recovery timescale after UPF. No trial has tracked recovery after stopping UPF specifically. Realistic framing: plant diversity (the "30 plants a week" idea comes from the observational American Gut Project), 30 g fibre, and some fermented foods (live yogurt, kefir, sauerkraut, kimchi) are the evidence-backed levers, with measurable shifts in weeks.

## 6. Sodium, potassium and blood pressure

UK adults average 8.4 g salt/day (men 9.2 g), 40% above the 6 g maximum; about 75% comes from processed food and takeaways, so home cooking is the main lever. DASH-pattern diets lower systolic BP by roughly 3 to 5 mmHg (larger in hypertensives and overweight adults). Potassium: each additional 780 mg/day or so lowers systolic BP 2 to 5 mmHg, mostly in hypertensives. Potassium-rich foods: potatoes, beans, lentils, bananas, spinach, tomatoes, yogurt, milk, fish.

Salt substitutes (75% sodium chloride, 25% potassium chloride): SSaSS (NEJM 2021, n=20,995, rural China) cut stroke 14%, major CV events 13%, death 12%, with no excess serious hyperkalaemia (CKD excluded). WHO issued a 2025 guideline endorsing them. UK products (LoSalt, supermarket reduced-sodium salt) are fine for a man with normal kidney function who is not on ACE inhibitors, ARBs, spironolactone or potassium-sparing diuretics. Check kidney function first if in doubt.

## 7. UK supermarket food list by nutrient (Aldi, Lidl, Tesco level; prices indicative 2025 to 2026)

- Fibre: porridge oats 1 kg 0.85 to 1.20; red lentils 500 g 0.99 to 1.40; tinned chickpeas and beans 0.45 to 0.60; wholemeal bread 0.60 to 1.00; frozen peas 900 g 0.75 to 1.00; frozen mixed veg or broccoli about 1.00; frozen berries about 2.00 per 400 g.
- Potassium and magnesium: potatoes (skin on), bananas, frozen spinach, tinned tomatoes, beans, lentils, yogurt.
- Omega-3, vitamin D, selenium: tinned sardines 120 g 0.40 to 0.60; tinned mackerel 0.65 to 0.90; tinned salmon 1.50 to 2.00; frozen salmon fillets.
- Iodine, calcium, protein: semi-skimmed milk 2 L about 1.50; Greek yogurt 1 kg 1.95 to 2.50; cottage cheese.
- Protein, B12, choline, selenium: eggs, 12 for 2.90 to 3.30. Up to one a day is not associated with heart disease or stroke in pooled cohorts (Drouin-Chartier 2020, BMJ, 28 cohorts, 1.7 million people, RR 0.99). UK guidance has no egg limit.
- Lean protein: chicken breast and thigh, frozen white fish, tinned tuna, tofu.
- Fats: olive and rapeseed oil, mixed nuts (portion-controlled at 1600 kcal), two Brazil nuts a day.
- Fermented: live natural yogurt, kefir 500 ml 1.50 to 2.00, sauerkraut or kimchi jar 1.50 to 3.00.
- Herbs, spices, lemon, vinegar, garlic to replace salt.

A day hitting most targets on about 1600 kcal: oats with milk and berries; lentil and vegetable soup with wholemeal bread; sardines on toast, or chicken with potatoes and two frozen veg; Greek yogurt with banana. Roughly 35 g fibre, 4000 mg potassium, 120 g protein, one oily fish portion.

## 8. Baseline blood tests to ask a UK GP for

After a year of poor diet at BMI 30: full blood count; HbA1c; lipid profile (non-fasting acceptable); liver function (ALT, AST, GGT; fatty liver disease affects up to 1 in 5 UK adults and is more common in men with BMI 30 or more; abnormal ALT should prompt a FIB-4 score); urea, electrolytes and eGFR (also useful before salt substitute or creatine); B12 and folate; TSH; blood pressure. Vitamin D: NICE CKS and most NHS formularies advise against routine testing without symptoms or specific indications; the GP will usually say "just take 10 ug", which is correct. Ferritin if the FBC shows anaemia.

NHS Health Check (England, 40 to 74, every 5 years, free, 20 to 30 min at GP or pharmacy): height, weight, BMI, waist, BP, cholesterol (often finger-prick), diabetes risk with HbA1c where indicated, alcohol and activity questions, family history, QRISK. It does not include liver, kidney, thyroid or vitamin tests; request those separately.
